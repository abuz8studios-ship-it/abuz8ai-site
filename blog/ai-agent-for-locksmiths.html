AI Agent for Locksmiths: Winning the 2am Call

Published August 10, 2026 · 8 min read

An AI agent for locksmiths earns its keep in one specific moment: 2:14am, someone is standing in a parking lot next to a locked car, and they are dialing the third number on the results page. Lockout customers do not leave voicemails. They do not wait for a callback. They hang up and dial again. Every ring you miss is a job that went to the shop that picked up, and you will never see it in any report because it never became a lead.

The math of a missed ring

Say you miss six calls a week — two while you are elbow deep in a mortise, two overnight, two while you are driving with a phone you are not going to answer. That is an illustrative number, not a study. Put your own average ticket next to it and the annual figure will annoy you more than anything else in this article.

The usual fix is an answering service. Those work, sort of. They take a name and a number, they cannot tell a residential lockout from a commercial master key rekey, and they hand you a message that still needs a call back. The person is already gone by then.

What the agent asks before you touch the van keys

A good intake for this trade is short. Nobody stranded in the cold is answering nine questions.

The six-question intake

  • Where exactly — street address or the cross streets and the lot. Half of lockout calls start with “the Walgreens.”
  • Car, house, or business? Different tools, different techs, different pricing.
  • Is anyone or any pet locked inside, and is anyone in danger? That answer changes the priority and, in some cases, means calling emergency services first.
  • Lock type as best they can describe it. Deadbolt, knob, push-button, high security, transponder key.
  • Can they prove they belong there? Say it early and gently, because they will need ID at the door either way.
  • Callback number, confirmed by a text they have to reply to.

By the time your phone buzzes you have a qualified job, an address that maps, and a customer who has already been told what happens next. You are deciding whether to roll, not conducting an interview at 2am.

Quoting on the phone without getting burned

Phone quoting is where locksmiths bleed. Give a firm number, arrive to a high-security cylinder, and now you are having an argument in a driveway in the dark. Refuse to give any number and the caller assumes you are the bait-and-switch outfit they read about.

The middle path is a service call fee stated plainly, a labor range, and the after-hours surcharge named before dispatch rather than discovered on the invoice. An agent delivers that identically every single time, which is more than most of us manage at 2am. Publish the same tiers on your own site while you are at it, for the customers who would rather not talk to anyone before they decide.

Say the surcharge before the truck moves. Almost every price dispute in this trade traces back to a number the customer heard for the first time after the work was done. An agent that reads the after-hours rate out loud during intake, and logs that it did, removes the argument entirely — and gives you a timestamped record when someone disputes the charge later.

The trust objection you cannot dodge

Your customer is about to let a stranger open their door at night. They are right to be suspicious. The trade has a reputation problem earned by out-of-state call centers subcontracting to whoever answers, and you pay for their behavior every day.

So make the agent handle it before it becomes a question. During intake it should state your licence number, your business name, that the tech will arrive in a marked vehicle, that ID will be requested at the door, and that the tech's name and photo will be texted before arrival. That last one costs nothing and changes the entire feel of a 2am arrival.

Afterwards, the same agent asks for the review while the relief is fresh — ideally within an hour of the door opening, not three days later. Route the good ones onto your site with an AI testimonial widget, because in this trade social proof is not vanity, it is the objection handler.

No-shows, ghosts, and the drive you wasted

Two failure modes cost real money. The customer who calls, gets a quote, keeps calling around, and is gone when you arrive. And the scheduled rekey where nobody is home.

Both are confirmation problems. The agent texts an ETA at dispatch, asks for a one-character reply to confirm the customer is still on site, and pings again if the tech gets held up. Silence becomes a signal you can act on before you burn forty minutes of drive time. For scheduled work it reminds the night before and again that morning.
