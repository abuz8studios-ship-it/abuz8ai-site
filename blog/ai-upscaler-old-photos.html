Restore Old Photos With AI: Upscaling Family History the Right Way

BUILDER NOTESAUG 11, 20266 MIN READ

Somewhere in your family there is a shoebox, and in it there's one photo that matters — a grandparent's wedding, the old house, someone who's gone. To restore old photos with AI well, you need to understand one thing before touching any tool: restoration is partly recovery and partly invention, and with the faces of people you love, the line between the two matters more than anywhere else in AI imaging. Our free AI image upscaler handles the recovery half. This post is the full craft.

The scan is the restoration

Everything downstream is limited by the scan. Phone-photographing a print adds glare, lens distortion, and your ceiling light to a damaged image. Do this instead:

  • Flatbed scanner at 600 DPI for prints (1200 DPI for small prints). Libraries often have free ones.
  • No scanner? Phone camera in indirect daylight near a window, print laid flat, camera parallel, shadows off. Take five shots, keep the sharpest.
  • Never scan through album plastic. Remove the print if it releases easily; if it's stuck, photograph at a slight angle to dodge the reflection, then correct perspective.
  • Save the raw scan untouched. That's the archival original — every experiment happens on copies.

What AI genuinely recovers

Upscaling models are trained on millions of photos, so they know what photographic grain, fabric, hair, and skin look like at higher resolution. Blur from a small or degraded print, softness from old lenses, grain amplified by time — these respond beautifully. A 2-inch faded print can come back as a sharp 8x10. Mild fading and color casts also correct well, because the information is still in the image, just compressed into a narrow tonal range.

What AI invents (and when to care)

Where the print is genuinely gone — a crease through a face, a torn corner, silverfish damage — the model doesn't recover, it guesses. For a background wall, a guess is fine; patch damage with the inpainting tool and nobody will ever know. For a face, be careful: aggressive "face enhancement" settings can quietly average your grandmother toward a generic face. The photo gets sharper and less her at the same time. The tell: compare eyes, nose bridge, and smile lines against the original at the same size. If a relative who knew them says "something's off," believe the relative over the algorithm.

The conservative workflow

1) Scan high. 2) Upscale 2x first, not 4x — inspect before going further; each step doubles both detail and invention. 3) Repair physical damage with inpainting, smallest masks possible. 4) Color-correct last, gently — a hundred-year-old photo that looks shot yesterday reads as fake; leave a little warmth and grain. 5) Export PNG, and store the original scan beside every edited version, labeled. You're the archivist now; future family will want the untouched file.

Colorizing black-and-white: a note

Colorization is pure invention — the model guesses that the dress was blue. It can be a beautiful gift, and it can also overwrite family memory ("no, that dress was green, I was there"). Best practice: present colorized versions as interpretations, always alongside the original. Never let the colorized copy become the only copy.
