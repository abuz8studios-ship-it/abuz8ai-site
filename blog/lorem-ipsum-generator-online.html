Here's the craft around the filler: when placeholder text helps, when it actively lies to you, and the small tricks that separate a clean mockup from a future rework.

Why nonsense beats real words

Placeholder text has one job: let people evaluate layout, typography, and spacing without reading. Real sentences sabotage that — stakeholders instinctively read them, and suddenly your typography review is a copy-editing meeting. Lorem ipsum's letter-pair distribution roughly resembles natural language, so line lengths, word rhythm, and paragraph texture look right, while the meaning stays out of the way. It's camouflage for the content layer. That neutrality is the feature, not laziness.

Match the shape, not just the volume

The most common filler mistake is volume mismatch. A headline slot filled with two words of lorem when the real headline will run to nine; a product description mocked at three tidy lines when the real ones sprawl to twelve. The layout "works" right up until real content arrives, then breaks in production. The fix costs nothing: generate filler in the actual expected sizes — short/medium/long variants of each field — and test the layout against all three. A generator with word- and sentence-level control exists precisely for this; paragraphs are for body copy, words are for labels and buttons.

When lorem ipsum starts lying

Honest limit: placeholder text is for evaluating form, and it quietly poisons decisions about content. If the design question is "does this pricing page persuade?" or "is this onboarding flow clear?", lorem can't answer — persuasion and clarity live in the words. Content-first design has a real point here: for high-stakes pages, draft rough-but-real copy early, because the copy is the design. The working rule: lorem for structure reviews, real words for meaning reviews, and never let a page ship with consectetur adipiscing still in a footer. (Everyone has done it once. Search your own site for "lorem" — we'll wait.)

How much filler does each slot need?

Rough sizing guide, learned from watching layouts break in production. Buttons and nav labels: one to two words — if your button copy wraps, the design was never tested honestly. Headlines: five to nine words, and mock the nine-word case, because someone in marketing will find it. Card blurbs and product descriptions: two to four sentences, with a long variant that tests truncation ("does the ellipsis appear, or does the card just grow forever?"). Body copy: two to three paragraphs per section is enough to judge rhythm; more just pads the scroll. Testimonials: mock the rambler, not the one-liner — real customers write four sentences with a comma splice, and your quote card should survive them. Generate each size once, paste into every instance of that component, and you've stress-tested the layout the way real content will.

Filler text in the AI era

One modern wrinkle: teams now sometimes use an LLM to generate "realistic" placeholder copy instead of lorem. Fine — but know what you're choosing. Realistic filler invites content feedback (sometimes you want that), burns tokens on throwaway text, and can smuggle plausible-but-wrong claims into a mockup that later gets screenshotted into a deck. Nonsense Latin has one unbeatable property: nobody has ever accidentally shipped it as a factual claim.
