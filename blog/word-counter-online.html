A word counter online looks like the simplest tool on the internet until you notice how much of modern writing is really limit management. The meta description that truncates at 160 characters. The post that dies at 280. The essay that must clear 650 words, the abstract that must not exceed 250, the ad headline with 30 characters of runway. Our free word counter counts words, characters, sentences, and reading time as you type, in your browser. The five-minute upgrade is knowing which meter each platform actually runs — because words, characters, and now tokens are three different currencies, and writers get charged in all three.

What counts as a word (the answer is a convention, not a law)

Every counter defines a word roughly the same way: characters separated by whitespace. "State-of-the-art" — one word. "A.I." — one word. A number, a URL, an em-dash-joined aside — the convention decides, which is why two counters can disagree by a handful on the same text. The disagreement almost never matters; limits have tolerance built in. What does matter is consistency: measure with the same counter you'll be judged by when the stakes are real (a submission portal, an ad platform), and use a fast local one for everything else. Precision theater about ±3 words is procrastination wearing a lab coat.

The limits that actually run your writing life

Characters rule the surfaces where space is physical: search snippets (titles ~60 characters, descriptions ~155–160 before the ellipsis eats your call to action), social posts, SMS, push notifications, ad headlines. Words rule the surfaces where attention is the budget: essays, articles, abstracts, cover letters. The failure mode is optimizing the wrong meter — polishing a meta description to a beautiful 40 words that Google truncates at character 158, mid-promise. Two habits fix it: know the meter before you write, and front-load the payload so truncation costs you style instead of substance. The first sentence should survive any cutoff with the point intact — a rule that improves writing even when nothing truncates.

Reading time: the honest metric hiding in the count

Reading-time estimates divide word count by average reading speed — silent adult prose reading clocks around 240 words per minute, so a 1,200-word piece reads in about five minutes. The number's real value isn't the badge on the post; it's the discipline before publishing. "Seven minutes" asks a hard question: have I earned seven minutes? Sometimes yes. Often it's a four-minute piece wearing three minutes of warm-up — and the counter catches it before your reader does. Same logic as a budget: the number isn't the goal, the number is what makes the tradeoffs visible. Cut the throat-clearing, keep the proof.

Tokens: the third meter, now that machines read too

The newest audience for your writing bills by neither words nor characters. LLMs meter tokens — vocabulary chunks averaging about 0.75 English words each — and the exchange rate shifts with content: prose runs near the average, code and structured data fragment into more tokens per visible character, other languages often cost multiples. If you write prompts, system messages, or anything an AI ingests at volume, the token meter is the one connected to an invoice. Rough-cut with words (750 words ≈ 1,000 tokens for English prose), then measure with the token counter when volume makes the difference material. Three meters, one text: characters for space, words for attention, tokens for compute.

Quick answers

Do "characters with spaces" or "without spaces" matter? Platforms with character limits count spaces — a space occupies a character slot everywhere it physically could. "Without spaces" survives mostly for certain translation and typesetting billing conventions. When someone says "160 characters," assume spaces count.

How many pages is 1,000 words? The old convention: about 4 double-spaced pages or 2 single-spaced, at standard 12-point type. It's an approximation from the print era — fonts, margins, and spacing swing it — which is exactly why limits moved to word counts in the first place.

Is there an ideal word count for SEO? No magic number — coverage beats length. Long posts often rank because they answer the question completely, not because they're long. Write until the question is answered, cut until nothing padding-shaped survives, and let the count land where it lands.

The bottom line

Words, characters, tokens — three meters, three different bills. Know which one your surface runs, front-load the payload for truncation, use reading time as an editing conscience, and count in a tool that doesn't harvest your drafts. The counter is simple; the awareness is the skill.
