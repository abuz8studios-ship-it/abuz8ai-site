The difference matters: old-fashioned resizing makes a bigger blurry image; AI upscaling makes a bigger sharp image by intelligently filling in texture that the original was too small to hold. Why "just resize it" doesn't work

When you drag the corner of a 500-pixel image up to 2000 pixels, your software has to invent 15 out of every 16 pixels. Classic resizing invents them by averaging neighbors — which is why the result looks soft and smeared. An AI upscaler invents them by recognizing what it's looking at: this is hair, so add hair texture; this is an eye, so sharpen the lashes; this is skin, so keep it smooth. It reconstructs detail that's consistent with the subject rather than blurring across it.

The honest version: the model is making an educated guess about detail that was never captured. For most photos that guess is excellent. For a few — fine print, a distant face — it's a plausible invention, not the truth. Knowing which is which is the whole skill.

How to get a clean result

Start with the best original you have

Upscaling amplifies everything, including JPEG compression artifacts. If you have a less-compressed version of the same image, use it. Garbage in scales up to bigger garbage.

Match the scale to the need

Don't jump to 4x out of habit. If you need a 2x size bump, run 2x — every doubling gives the model more room to invent, and more invention means more risk of the plastic look. Use the smallest enlargement that solves your problem.

Check faces and text at 100%

These are where AI upscalers slip. Zoom into faces and any text after upscaling. If a face looks waxy or text turned into gibberish, that region was guessed — re-run at a lower scale or accept that the detail simply wasn't there to recover.

The honesty pass

What it does well: rescues small-but-good photos, restores soft scans, and beats classic resizing in every case where the subject is recognizable. There's a ceiling on how much real information can be recovered, and past it the model is painting, not restoring.
