AI Wedding Planner (Free): Plan the Whole Day Without Losing Your Mind

LIFEJULY 22, 20265 MIN READ

A free AI wedding planner won't taste your cake or talk your cousins out of a group dance. What it will do is the part that actually breaks people: the planning overhead. The 200-line checklist, the budget that has to split one number across fourteen vendors, the day-of timeline where the photographer, the caterer, and golden hour all have opinions. That's coordination work — and coordination work is exactly what AI is good at doing in thirty seconds instead of thirty browser tabs.

Here's how to use one well, what to let it decide, and — just as important — what to never delegate to it.

What an AI wedding planner actually produces

Give a good planner tool your date, guest count, budget, and style, and you should get four concrete artifacts back. A checklist ordered by deadline, not by category — "book the venue" belongs twelve months out, "confirm final headcount" belongs at two weeks, and mixing those up is how panic happens. A budget split across venue, catering, photography, attire, flowers, music, and the miscellaneous line everyone forgets. A day-of timeline from hair-and-makeup to last dance, with buffer built in. And a set of vendor questions — the ones you don't know to ask until it's too late, like "is there an overtime rate?" and "who's the backup if you're sick?"

The budget math, illustrated

Say the number is $20,000 for 100 guests — an example, not a benchmark; the tool re-does this arithmetic for whatever your number is. A sane starting split puts roughly 40% into venue and catering (about $8,000 — it's the biggest line for almost everyone), 12% into photography, 10% into attire, 8% each into flowers and music, and holds a real 10% contingency (that's $2,000) for the overruns that always come. The point isn't that those percentages are sacred — it's that every dollar has a job before you start booking, so when the florist quote comes in high, you're consciously trading against the music budget instead of quietly torching the contingency in month two.

How to prompt it like you mean it

The output is only as good as the constraints you feed it. "Plan my wedding" gets you a generic magazine listicle. "Outdoor ceremony for 80 in northern Ohio in October, $15k all-in, no dancing, halal catering, photography matters more than flowers" gets you a plan that's actually yours. Feed it your non-negotiables first, your nice-to-haves second, and tell it what you don't care about — that's where it'll find your budget slack.

What the AI shouldn't decide

Straight talk: don't outsource judgment. The AI doesn't know that your two friends can't sit at the same table, that your grandmother needs the aisle seat near the exit, or that "simple ceremony" means something specific in your family. It also can't vet a vendor's character — read the contracts yourself, meet people, trust your gut. Use the AI for structure, sequence, and completeness. Keep taste, people, and money decisions human. It's your day; the tool just carries the clipboard.

The timeline, built backwards

Good timelines are built from the date, backwards. Twelve months out is for the big three: venue, photographer, caterer — the vendors who book out first and constrain everything else. Six months out is the middle band: attire ordered (alterations need runway), music booked, invitations designed. Three months out: menu locked, timeline drafted, vendor contracts re-read with fresh eyes. One month: final headcount to the caterer, seating chart (the diplomatic phase), and confirmation calls to every vendor — not emails, calls. The week of: delegate everything with a name attached, because on the day itself the only job you should hold is showing up. An AI planner generates exactly this cascade from your date in seconds, which matters less for the list itself and more for what it prevents: discovering in month ten that a month-twelve task existed.

Free vs. paid planning tools

The paid wedding-planning apps mostly sell you the same checklist wrapped in vendor advertising — the "recommended" florist is often a paid placement, which means the tool's incentives aren't yours. A free planner with no signup has a cleaner deal: you type, it plans, nobody's upselling you a videographer.
