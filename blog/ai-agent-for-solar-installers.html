AI Agent for Solar Installers: The Quote Request You Never Answered

Published August 10, 2026 · 8 min read

An AI agent for solar installers is the crew member who answers the 9:47pm quote request while you are on a ladder in the dark or asleep with your phone face down. Solar sells on momentum. A homeowner sees their July bill, watches two videos, fills out three forms in twenty minutes, and signs with whoever replies first. Not the cheapest. The first. If that is not you, the deposit went somewhere else and you never even knew the lead existed.

The day you actually have

You are on site at seven. Racking, inverters, conduit, the one junction box that refuses to cooperate. Meanwhile the phone stacks up: four form fills, two texts asking “how much for 8kW,” the AHJ wanting a revised single-line diagram, a March customer asking whether their interconnection ever went through, and a subcontractor who is not coming tomorrow.

You get to all of it at eight at night, exhausted, and you triage it badly. Everyone triages badly at eight at night. That is not a discipline problem. It is a physics problem — you were holding a drill for eleven hours.

An agent does not fix your roof work. It fixes the eleven hours where nothing happens because your hands were busy.

Reply in ninety seconds, not nine hours

Speed to first reply is the whole ballgame in residential solar, because the homeowner filled out three forms, not one. Your agent watches the form inbox, the missed calls, and the texts. It replies immediately — by the channel they used — acknowledges the request by name, and starts qualifying instead of saying “someone will be in touch.”

The five questions that decide whether a lead is real

  • Do they own the home? A renter cannot sign, and no amount of enthusiasm changes that.
  • What is the average monthly electric bill? Below a certain number the payback math simply does not work and you both save a Saturday.
  • Roof age and material. A 22-year-old composite roof is a re-roof conversation before it is a solar conversation.
  • Orientation and shade. Two mature oaks on the south side change the entire proposal.
  • Cash, loan, lease, or “I have not thought about it”? That last answer is not a disqualifier, but it tells you which call to have.

Five questions, asked conversationally over text, answered while the homeowner is still excited. By morning you have a sorted list instead of a pile.

Quote a range, never a number

This is where a careless agent does damage. It should never invent a firm price. What works is a band with the assumptions said out loud: systems this size in your area typically land in a certain range before incentives, and the real number depends on the roof, the main panel, and your utility. Then it books the site visit.

That band does two jobs. It ends the conversation with the person expecting a whole-home system for four thousand dollars, which is a mercy for both of you. And it keeps the serious buyer from drifting to a competitor who was willing to talk money.

The rule for any quoting agent: ranges are fine, promises are not. An agent that invents a firm price you then have to walk back on the site visit costs you more trust than the lead was ever worth. Give a band, name the assumptions, hand the real number to a human who has seen the roof.

Financing questions, answered the same way every time

Half of solar conversations are not about panels. Loan versus lease versus PPA. What happens when they sell the house. What the federal credit is and, more importantly, what it is not. You have explained this four hundred times and by the fourth hundred you explain it badly.

An agent explains it identically at eleven at night, in the same words you approved, and knows where the line is: it hands off anything that sounds like tax advice to you and says so plainly rather than guessing. Consistency here is worth more than eloquence.

The long middle nobody manages

Signature to permission to operate is not a week. It is often two to four months of AHJ review, utility interconnection, HOA approval, and inspection scheduling. Customers go quiet, then anxious, then they call to ask if they have been forgotten — and some of them cancel out of pure silence.

Give that middle to the agent. One unprompted text a week: permit is with the city, submitted Tuesday, expect two to three weeks, nothing needed from you. It costs nothing and it prevents most of the panic calls. In the same loop the agent can draft the boring paper: permit checklists, revision cover letters, HOA submissions, inspection confirmations, and the customer-facing summary of each. Seasonal spikes without seasonal hiring

Solar leads do not arrive evenly. A heat wave, a rate-hike announcement, an incentive deadline — and suddenly three weeks look like a different business, followed by a month where the phone barely rings. Hiring an office person for the spike is a bad trade, and so is dropping half the spike on the floor.

An agent does not care whether today brought two leads or forty. Same reply time either way. That is the actual argument for it: not that it is smarter than your coordinator, but that it does not have a capacity ceiling on the week that matters most.

The referral loop you keep meaning to build

Your best lead is the neighbor who watched a crew work on that street for two days. Ask for the review at permission to operate, not at install — ask when the meter is spinning backward and the customer is delighted, not when they are still waiting. An agent can time that request precisely, then route the good ones onto your site with an AI testimonial widget and turn the best ones into a door-knock list for that whole street.
