Writing a Eulogy When the Words Won't Come

WRITING HELPJULY 22, 20265 MIN READ

If you're reading this, someone has probably asked you to speak — at exactly the moment when speaking feels impossible. First, the thing nobody says clearly enough: a eulogy is not a performance, and it is not graded. The people in the room are not waiting for eloquence. They're waiting to hear the person they loved described by someone who knew them. If you stand up and say three true things in a shaking voice, you will have done it right.

This guide walks through a simple structure, offers a way to find your material, and explains honestly where an AI eulogy writer can help — and where it shouldn't.

A structure that holds you up

You don't need an original shape. Borrow this one. Open with who they were to you — "I'm Sarah, and Marcus was my older brother" does more work than any polished opening line, because it tells the room why you're the one standing there. Then tell two or three specific stories. Not summaries of their character — moments. The time they drove four hours because you sounded off on the phone. The way they argued with referees on television. Specific is what makes a room laugh and cry at the same time; general is what makes a eulogy feel like a résumé. Then say what they taught you, or what you'll carry — one thing is enough. Then say goodbye in whatever words are yours. Four movements, three to five minutes spoken. Shorter is not a failure; it never was.

Finding the stories

Don't start by writing. Start by remembering, with a pen nearby. What did they always say? What made them laugh until they couldn't breathe? What small thing did they do every single time — the coffee order, the terrible parking, the way they answered the phone? Text a few people who loved them and ask for one memory each; you'll get material no one else could have given you, and the asking itself is a kindness to the people you ask. You are not looking for the perfect story. You're looking for the true ones.

Where an AI helper honestly fits

Grief does a specific cruel thing to writing: you know everything and can say nothing. The blank page becomes another loss. This is the narrow place where the AI eulogy writer is built to help — you give it the raw, unpolished pieces (their name, your relationship, the memories and phrases you jotted down), and it hands you a structured first draft. Not so you can read a machine's words at a funeral — so you have something to push against. Editing is easier than starting. You'll read the draft and immediately know what's wrong with it — "that's not how she talked," "it's missing the lake house" — and every one of those corrections is you finding your own eulogy. Rewrite it until it sounds like you talking about them. The words you keep should be yours; the draft just gets you off the blank page.

Delivering it

Print it large — 16-point type or bigger, double-spaced — because trembling hands and small fonts are a bad pair. Read it aloud at least twice beforehand; you'll find the sentence that always cracks you, and knowing where it lives means it won't ambush you. Pauses are allowed. Crying is allowed — pause, breathe, keep going; no one in that room is timing you. And if you truly can't finish, it's okay to hand the page to someone or simply say "you all know the rest." Standing up was the tribute.

A last permission slip

You will forget a story you meant to tell. You will think of the perfect line on the drive home. That's not failure — that's proof there was more to the person than an afternoon could hold. The eulogy isn't the last word on them; it's one voice, on one day, doing its best. That has always been enough.
